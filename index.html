The 12 rules you must not forget

1. FACTOR FIRST Before simplifying complicated algebraic fractions.

2. COMMON DENOMINATOR Before adding or subtracting fractions.

3. RECIPROCAL When dividing fractions, flip the second fraction.

4. NEGATIVE INEQUALITY Multiply or divide by a negative → reverse the inequality sign.

5. STATIONARY POINT Differentiate, set derivative = 0, solve, substitute into original function.

6. PROBABILITY AND along a path = multiply; OR between separate cases = add.

7. WITHOUT REPLACEMENT Reduce the total and relevant quantities after each selection.

8. MATRICES Multiply row by column; AB usually differs from BA.

9. FUNCTIONS fg(x) means apply g first, then f.

10. QUADRATICS Factorise or use the quadratic formula; check both roots.

11. GEOMETRY Write reasons, not just angle values. Never assume the diagram is to scale.

12. FINAL ANSWER Check units, restrictions, signs, rounding and every requested result.

1. Algebraic fractions — hardest expressions

High-priority method

Whenever you see huge algebraic fractions, cubics, repeated brackets, or denominators such as \((3x-2)^2\), follow this sequence.

FACTOR → REWRITE → CANCEL → SIMPLIFY → CHECK RESTRICTIONS

Recognise these identities instantly

ExpressionFactorised form
\(a^2-b^2\)\((a-b)(a+b)\)
\(a^2+2ab+b^2\)\((a+b)^2\)
\(a^2-2ab+b^2\)\((a-b)^2\)
\(a^3-b^3\)\((a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)\)
\(a^3+b^3\)\((a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)\)
\(x^2+(p+q)x+pq\)\((x+p)(x+q)\)

Remember: minus between two cubes produces plus in the middle of the quadratic factor.

Worked example: a large fraction with cubes

Simplify:

\[ \frac{27x^3-8}{9x^2-12x+4}\times\frac{3x-2}{3x+1} \]

Step 1 — Factor both expressions.

\[ 27x^3-8=(3x-2)(9x^2+6x+4) \]

\[ 9x^2-12x+4=(3x-2)^2 \]

Step 2 — Rewrite the entire fraction.

\[ \frac{(3x-2)(9x^2+6x+4)}{(3x-2)^2} \times\frac{3x-2}{3x+1} \]

Step 3 — Cancel common factors.

There are two factors of \((3x-2)\) above and two below.

\[ \boxed{\frac{9x^2+6x+4}{3x+1}} \]

Restrictions from the original denominator:

\[ x\ne\frac23,\qquad x\ne-\frac13 \]

Never forget:

  • \((3x-2)(3x-2)=(3x-2)^2\), not \(9x^2-4\).
  • You cancel factors, not individual terms joined by addition or subtraction.
  • You may cancel \((x+2)\) against \((x+2)\), but cannot cancel \(x\) from \(\frac{x+2}{x+3}\).
  • A factor removed by cancellation still gives an excluded value of \(x\).

Adding and subtracting algebraic fractions

Example:

\[ \frac{2}{3x-2}-\frac{x+1}{(3x-2)^2} \]

The lowest common denominator is \((3x-2)^2\).

\[ =\frac{2(3x-2)-(x+1)}{(3x-2)^2} \]

\[ =\boxed{\frac{5x-5}{(3x-2)^2}} \]

Watch the minus sign: \(-(x+1)=-x-1\).

Rules for every operation:

OperationWhat to do
AdditionFind common denominator, then add numerators
SubtractionFind common denominator, subtract the entire numerator
MultiplicationFactor, cancel, multiply
DivisionFlip second fraction, then multiply
Solving an equationState restrictions, clear denominators, solve and check

2. Quadratics, cubics and equations

Quadratic formula

For \(ax^2+bx+c=0\):

\[ \boxed{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}} \]

Discriminant \(D=b^2-4ac\):

  • \(D>0\): two different real roots.
  • \(D=0\): one repeated real root.
  • \(D<0\): no real roots.

Completing the square

\[ x^2+bx+c= \left(x+\frac b2\right)^2+c-\frac{b^2}{4} \]

Example:

\[ x^2-6x+5=(x-3)^2-4 \]

Technique: halve the coefficient of \(x\), square it, then adjust the constant.

For \(ax^2+bx+c\), factor out \(a\) from the first two terms before completing the square.

Cubic equations and factor theorem

For a cubic \(f(x)\):

  1. Test a possible root \(r\).
  2. If \(f(r)=0\), then \((x-r)\) is a factor.
  3. Divide the cubic by \((x-r)\).
  4. Factorise or solve the remaining quadratic.

For \((ax-b)\), test \(x=b/a\), not \(x=b\).

Important identities:

\[ x^3-y^3=(x-y)(x^2+xy+y^2) \]

\[ x^3+y^3=(x+y)(x^2-xy+y^2) \]

Simultaneous equations

Two linear equations: elimination or substitution.

One linear + one quadratic:

  1. Rearrange the linear equation.
  2. Substitute into the quadratic.
  3. Expand and collect terms.
  4. Solve the resulting quadratic.
  5. Substitute both answers into the linear equation.

Never give one solution if the quadratic produces two valid pairs.

Inequalities

For \(x^2-5x+4<0\):

\[ (x-1)(x-4)<0 \]

Roots are \(1\) and \(4\). Since the parabola opens upwards, it is negative between the roots.

\[ \boxed{1<x<4} \]

Remember:

  • \(\lt,\gt\): open-circle endpoints.
  • \(\le,\ge\): filled-circle endpoints.
  • Divide by a negative: reverse the inequality.
  • Quadratic inequalities: find roots, then use signs or a sketch.

Shaded regions: rearrange to \(y\), use solid lines for inclusive inequalities, dashed lines for strict ones, then test a point such as \((0,0)\) if it is not on the boundary.

WHEN TO USE IT · WORKED EXAMPLE

Equations · choose the method

Factorise when roots are easy; use the formula when they are not. For x² − 5x + 6 = 0, (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0, so x = 2 or 3. For a cubic, f(x) = x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 has f(1) = 0, so divide by x − 1 to get x² − 5x + 6; roots are 1, 2, 3. Simultaneous example: y = x + 1 and y = x² − 1 give x² − x − 2 = 0, so (x,y) = (2,3) or (−1,0).

3. Differentiation — stationary points, tangents and normals

Essential

The power rule

\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}(ax^n)=anx^{n-1}} \]

Examples:

OriginalDerivative
\(x^4\)\(4x^3\)
\(5x^3\)\(15x^2\)
\(-7x^2\)\(-14x\)
\(6x\)\(6\)
\(12\)\(0\)
\(x^{-2}\)\(-2x^{-3}\)

Differentiate every term separately. Constants become zero.

Stationary points — full technique

Given:

\[ y=x^3-6x^2+9x+1 \]

Step 1 — Differentiate.

\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=3x^2-12x+9 \]

Step 2 — Set derivative equal to zero.

\[ 3x^2-12x+9=0 \]

\[ 3(x-1)(x-3)=0 \]

Thus \(x=1\) or \(x=3\).

Step 3 — Substitute into the ORIGINAL equation.

\[ y(1)=5,\quad y(3)=1 \]

Stationary points are:

\[ \boxed{(1,5)\text{ and }(3,1)} \]

Step 4 — Classify them.

Use the second derivative:

\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=6x-12 \]

  • At \(x=1\), second derivative is negative → local maximum.
  • At \(x=3\), second derivative is positive → local minimum.

Therefore \((1,5)\) is a maximum and \((3,1)\) is a minimum.

If the second derivative is zero, this test is inconclusive; check the gradient's sign on both sides.

Tangents and normals

The derivative at a point is the tangent gradient.

For a tangent through \((x_1,y_1)\):

\[ \boxed{y-y_1=m(x-x_1)} \]

The normal is perpendicular:

\[ m_{\text{normal}}=-\frac{1}{m_{\text{tangent}}} \]

If the tangent gradient is zero, the normal is vertical.

Technique: differentiate → substitute the given \(x\) → find the gradient → calculate \(y\) → use the straight-line formula.

Motion questions

\[ v=\frac{ds}{dt},\qquad a=\frac{dv}{dt} \]

  • Displacement → differentiate → velocity.
  • Velocity → differentiate → acceleration.
  • At rest → set \(v=0\).
  • Speed is the magnitude \(|v|\), not negative velocity.
  • Distance–time gradient = speed.
  • Displacement–time gradient = velocity.
  • Velocity–time gradient = acceleration.
  • Area under velocity–time graph = displacement; account for negative regions.
  • Area under speed–time graph = distance.

Do not forget: Stationary means zero gradient. It does not automatically mean maximum or minimum.

WHEN TO USE IT · WORKED EXAMPLE

Tangents · use the derivative at a point

For y = x² at x = 2: y = 4 and dy/dx = 2x = 4. Tangent: y − 4 = 4(x − 2), giving y = 4x − 4. Normal gradient = −¼, so y − 4 = −¼(x − 2). For motion s = t³ − 3t, v = 3t² − 3 and a = 6t; at rest at t = 1 for t ≥ 0.

With and without replacement

A bag contains 3 red and 2 blue balls.

With replacement: probabilities remain unchanged after the first draw.

\[ P(RR)=\frac35\times\frac35=\frac9{25} \]

Without replacement: the denominator decreases because one ball is gone.

\[ P(RR)=\frac35\times\frac24=\frac3{10} \]

For exactly one red:

\[ P(RB)+P(BR) \]

\[ =\frac35\times\frac24+\frac25\times\frac34 =\boxed{\frac35} \]

For at least one red:

\[ 1-P(BB)=1-\frac25\times\frac14 =\boxed{\frac9{10}} \]

Remember: For tree diagrams, multiply along each complete path, then add the separate paths that satisfy the question.

Conditional probability

If the question says "given that the first ball was red", the first selection is already known.

\[ P(\text{second red given first red})=\frac24 \]

Do not multiply by \(3/5\) again.

For two events:

\[ P(A\text{ given }B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} \]

Other probability traps

  • Independent does not mean mutually exclusive.
  • Probabilities of all possible outcomes sum to 1.
  • For a fair six-sided die, each individual number has probability \(1/6\).
  • "At least two" includes two, three, four, etc.
  • "More than two" excludes two.
  • For unordered pairs, count both possible orders unless the problem's outcomes are already defined as unordered.

🎲 Complex probability explained · choose the right event

Start here: write exactly which outcomes count. “And” means follow one complete path; multiply the probability at each step, using the information already known. “Or” means combine cases; add only disjoint cases, or subtract the overlap.

1. Without replacement · exactly two successes

Example: a bag contains 4 red and 3 blue balls. Draw 3 without replacement. Find the probability of exactly 2 reds.

The successful orders are RRB, RBR and BRR. The total falls 7 → 6 → 5 on every path.

\[P(RRB)=\frac47\cdot\frac36\cdot\frac35=\frac6{35}.\]

\[P(RBR)=\frac47\cdot\frac36\cdot\frac35=\frac6{35},\quad P(BRR)=\frac37\cdot\frac46\cdot\frac35=\frac6{35}.\]

Add the three separate orders: \(P(\text{exactly 2 red})=18/35\). They have the same numerical value here, but the branch labels and counts differ. Listing just RRB misses two valid orders.

2. “At least one” · use the opposite

Example: with the same bag and draws, “at least one red” means 1, 2 or 3 reds. Its opposite is no reds: BBB.

\[P(\text{at least 1 red})=1-P(BBB)=1-\frac37\cdot\frac26\cdot\frac15=\frac{34}{35}.\]

When useful: the unwanted event has fewer paths than the wanted event. “At least two” has a different complement: zero or one.

3. Conditional probability · shrink the sample space

Example: out of 30 students, 18 study French, 12 study Spanish and 7 study both. Given a student studies French, find the probability they study Spanish.

Only the 18 French students remain eligible; 7 also study Spanish. Hence \(P(S\mid F)=7/18\). By contrast, \(P(F\cap S)=7/30\) and \(P(F\cup S)=(18+12-7)/30=23/30\).

Trap: “given French” changes the denominator. Also, independent events satisfy \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\), provided \(P(B)>0\); mutually exclusive events cannot happen together.

4. A hidden starting choice · work backwards

Example: choose bag A or B with probability ½ each. A has 3 red and 1 blue; B has 1 red and 3 blue. One ball is drawn and is red. What is the probability bag A was used?

\[P(A\cap R)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac34=\tfrac38,\quad P(B\cap R)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac14=\tfrac18.\]

The red total is ⅜ + ⅛ = ½. So \(P(A\mid R)=(3/8)/(1/2)=3/4\). Restrict attention to the red-ending paths, then find A’s share.

Practice · 3 questions
  1. A bag has 5 red and 3 blue balls. Two are drawn without replacement. Find the probability of exactly one red.
  2. A fair die is rolled 3 independent times. Find the probability of at least one six.
  3. Of 40 students, 24 play football and 10 of those also swim. Given a student plays football, find the probability they swim.
Reveal worked answers
  1. RB + BR = (5/8)(3/7) + (3/8)(5/7) = 30/56 = 15/28.
  2. 1 − P(no sixes) = 1 − (5/6)³ = 91/216.
  3. The football group is the sample space: 10/24 = 5/12.

5. Matrices — every essential operation

Let

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \]

OperationRule
AdditionAdd matching positions
SubtractionSubtract matching positions
Scalar multiplicationMultiply every entry
Matrix multiplicationRow × column
Determinant\(ad-bc\)
Inverse\(\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}\)

Matrix multiplication

\[ \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}e&f\\g&h\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ae+bg&af+bh\\\ ce+dg&cf+dh \end{pmatrix} \]

Never multiply corresponding entries for ordinary matrix multiplication.

  • \(AB\ne BA\) in general.
  • \(A(B+C)=AB+AC\).
  • \(AI=IA=A\).
  • \(A^{-1}A=I\).
  • \(A^{-1}\) exists for a \(2\times2\) matrix only when \(ad-bc\ne0\).
  • The number of columns of the first matrix must equal the number of rows of the second.

Solving matrix equations: If \(AX=B\), multiply on the left by \(A^{-1}\), giving \(X=A^{-1}B\).

Transformation matrices

TransformationMatrix

For combined transformations, the right-hand matrix acts first.

WHEN TO USE IT · WORKED EXAMPLE

Matrices · row by column in action

Let A = [(1, 2), (3, 4)] and B = [(2, 0), (1, 3)] (each pair is a row). AB = [(4, 6), (10, 12)]. The top-left entry is 1×2 + 2×1 = 4. det(A) = 1×4 − 2×3 = −2, so A⁻¹ = −½[(4, −2), (−3, 1)]. A 90° anticlockwise rotation sends (2,3) to (−3,2).

🔢 Matrices explained · order, inverses and transformations

Start here: a matrix organises numbers into rows and columns. In multiplication, each answer entry is the dot product of one whole row from the first matrix and one whole column from the second.

1. Dimensions · can you multiply?

\[(m\times n)(n\times p)\longrightarrow m\times p.\]

The inner dimensions must agree; the outer dimensions give the answer size. A 2×3 matrix can multiply a 3×1 column vector, producing 2×1. Reversing that order would require 1 = 2, so that reversed product is undefined.

2. Multiplication · calculate every entry

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\3&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

\[AB=\begin{pmatrix}2(1)+1(0)&2(4)+1(-1)\\3(1)+2(0)&3(4)+2(-1)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&7\\3&10\end{pmatrix}.\]

But \(BA=\begin{pmatrix}14&9\\-3&-2\end{pmatrix}\). Both products exist, yet their values differ. Trap: ordinary multiplication is not entry-by-entry multiplication, and you cannot swap the order.

3. Inverses · undo a transformation

For A above, det(A) = 2×2 − 1×3 = 1. Swap the main-diagonal entries, negate the other two, then divide by the determinant:

\[A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-3&2\end{pmatrix}.\]

Use to solve: 2x + y = 5 and 3x + 2y = 8 become \(A\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}5\\8\end{pmatrix}\). Multiply on the left by A⁻¹:

\[\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-3&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}5\\8\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}10-8\\-15+16\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Check: 2(2)+1=5 and 3(2)+2(1)=8. If det(A)=0, A has no inverse; this method cannot be used. In a matrix equation AX=B, write X=A⁻¹B, preserving the order.

4. Combined transformations · rightmost acts first

Let \(R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) be a 90° anticlockwise rotation and \(F=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\) a reflection in the x-axis. Using column vectors, “rotate, then reflect” is FR.

\[FR=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.\]

On (2,1): rotation gives (−1,2), then reflection gives (−1,−2). The combined matrix gives the same result. Reverse the actions and RF sends (2,1) to (1,2), so order matters.

5. Find a transformation from unit vectors

The images of (1,0) and (0,1) are the first and second columns. If they map to (2,1) and (−1,3), the matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\). It maps (4,2) to (6,10).

Practice · 3 questions
  1. Find the inverse of C = [(3,1),(2,1)], where each pair is a row.
  2. Use that inverse to solve 3x + y = 7 and 2x + y = 5.
  3. A transformation maps (1,0) to (0,−1) and (0,1) to (1,0). Write its matrix, name it, and find the image of (3,2).
Reveal worked answers
  1. det(C)=3−2=1. C⁻¹ = [(1,−1),(−2,3)].
  2. x = 7−5=2; y = −2(7)+3(5)=1. Check both equations.
  3. Matrix [(0,1),(−1,0)]: 90° clockwise rotation about the origin. Image = (2,−3).

6. Functions, sequences and graphs

Function notation

If \(f(x)=2x+3\), then \(f(4)=11\).

For composite functions:

\[ fg(x)=f(g(x)) \]

Always do the inside function first.

For an inverse:

  1. Replace \(f(x)\) with \(y\).
  2. Swap \(x\) and \(y\).
  3. Rearrange for \(y\).
  4. Write \(f^{-1}(x)\).

Domain: permissible input values. Range: possible outputs.

Exclude any value making a denominator zero.

Straight-line graphs

\[ y=mx+c,\qquad m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} \]

  • Parallel lines: same gradient.
  • Perpendicular lines: product of gradients \(=-1\), when both gradients are defined.
  • At the intersection: both equations have the same \(x,y\).
  • x-intercept: set \(y=0\).
  • y-intercept: set \(x=0\).

Sequences

Arithmetic sequence:

\[ u_n=a+(n-1)d \]

Where \(a\) is the first term and \(d\) is the common difference.

For quadratic sequences, find the constant second difference:

\[ u_n=an^2+bn+c \]

The constant second difference is \(2a\).

Proportion

  • \(y\propto x\) → \(y=kx\)
  • \(y\propto x^2\) → \(y=kx^2\)
  • \(y\propto 1/x\) → \(y=k/x\)
  • \(y\propto 1/x^2\) → \(y=k/x^2\)

Find \(k\) first, using the supplied values.

WHEN TO USE IT · WORKED EXAMPLE

Functions, sequences & proportion · quick applications

If f(x) = 2x + 3 and g(x) = x², fg(2) = f(4) = 11, while gf(2) = g(7) = 49. f⁻¹(x) = (x − 3)/2. Sequence 5,8,11,… has uₙ = 3n + 2. Sequence 2,5,10,17,… has second difference 2, so uₙ = n² + 1. If y ∝ x² and y = 12 when x = 2, k = 3; when x = 5, y = 75.

7. Geometry and trigonometry — formula recap

Trigonometry

\[ \sin\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \]

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \]

\[ \tan\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} \]

For non-right triangles:

\[ \frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C} \]

\[ a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A \]

\[ \text{Area}=\frac12 ab\sin C \]

Remember:

  • Sine rule: opposite side–angle pairs.
  • Cosine rule: three sides, or two sides and their included angle.
  • Check the ambiguous case when using the sine rule to find an angle.
  • Calculator must be in DEG mode.
  • Bearings are measured clockwise from North, using three figures.

  • SSA generally does not prove congruence.

WHEN TO USE IT · WORKED EXAMPLE

Geometry · match the formula to the clues

Right triangle: opposite = 3, hypotenuse = 5 → θ = sin⁻¹(3/5) ≈ 36.9°. Two sides 5 and 7 with included angle 60° → opposite side² = 25 + 49 − 70cos60° = 39, so side = √39. 8. Vectors

For points \(A\) and \(B\):

\[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf b-\mathbf a \]

For \(\mathbf v=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\):

\[ |\mathbf v|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} \]

Unit vector:

\[ \frac{\mathbf v}{|\mathbf v|} \]

Vector proof rules:

  • Parallel vectors are scalar multiples.
  • Collinear points lie on the same straight line.
  • A midpoint divides a vector into two equal vectors.
  • If \(M\) divides \(AB\) in the ratio \(p:q\), then

\[ \overrightarrow{AM}=\frac{p}{p+q}\overrightarrow{AB} \]

Remember: Draw arrows and follow direction. \(\overrightarrow{BA}=-\overrightarrow{AB}\).

WHEN TO USE IT · WORKED EXAMPLE

Vectors · direction, ratio & proof

For A = (1,2), B = (7,10), AB = (6,8), length = 10 and unit vector = (3/5,4/5). If AM:MB = 1:2, then AM = ⅓AB = (2,8/3), so M = (3,14/3). To prove three points collinear, show vectors from one common point are scalar multiples.

🛣️ The Fundamental Laws of Vector Mapping

Use when: a diagram gives some arrows and asks you to express another journey in terms of a and b. Vectors describe displacement: choose a route, follow the arrows, and add each leg.

  • Join routes: \(\overrightarrow{AC}=\overrightarrow{AB}+\overrightarrow{BC}\).
  • Reverse an arrow: \(\overrightarrow{BA}=-\overrightarrow{AB}\).
  • Position vectors: destination minus start: \(\overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf b-\mathbf a\).
  • Scale an arrow: \(k\mathbf a\) multiplies its length by \(|k|\); a negative value reverses direction.
  • Closed journey: \(\overrightarrow{AB}+\overrightarrow{BC}+\overrightarrow{CA}=\mathbf0\).
WORKED EXAMPLE · CHOOSE A ROUTE

Given \(\overrightarrow{OA}=2\mathbf a\) and \(\overrightarrow{OB}=3\mathbf b\), travel A → O → B:

\[\overrightarrow{AB}=-2\mathbf a+3\mathbf b.\]

If M is the midpoint of AB, start at O → A → M:

\[\overrightarrow{OM}=2\mathbf a+\tfrac12(-2\mathbf a+3\mathbf b)=\mathbf a+\tfrac32\mathbf b.\]

Trap: OA + OB is not AB. The first leg is AO, so reverse OA.

Try it 1 · Route and midpoint

Question: OA = 3a and OB = 2b. Find AB and the position vector of midpoint M of AB.

Reveal worked answer

AB = AO + OB = −3a + 2b. OM = OA + ½AB = 3a + ½(−3a + 2b) = ³⁄₂a + b.

🗝️ The “Secret Treasure” Strategy · Collinearity / Straight Lines

Use when: asked to prove three points lie on one straight line. The “treasure” is finding the same bracket hidden in two vectors.

  1. Choose two vectors with a common point, such as AB and AC.
  2. Express both using the same base vectors.
  3. Factor out a number to show \(\overrightarrow{AC}=k\overrightarrow{AB}\).
  4. State the conclusion: the vectors are parallel and share A, so A, B and C are collinear.
WORKED EXAMPLE · SPOT THE SAME BRACKET

Given \(\overrightarrow{AB}=2\mathbf a-\mathbf b\) and \(\overrightarrow{AC}=6\mathbf a-3\mathbf b\):

\[\overrightarrow{AC}=3(2\mathbf a-\mathbf b)=3\overrightarrow{AB}.\]

Both start at A, so A, B and C lie on one straight line. AC is three times AB in the same direction, so B lies between A and C and AB:BC = 1:2.

Trap: parallel arrows on different lines do not prove three named points are collinear. Include the shared-point reason.

Try it 2 · Prove a straight line

Question: OA = a, OB = b, and OC = 4b − 3a. Prove A, B, C are collinear and find AB:BC.

Reveal worked answer

AB = b − a. AC = OC − OA = 4b − 4a = 4(b − a) = 4AB. They share A, so the points are collinear. BC = AC − AB = 3AB, hence AB:BC = 1:3.

🧩 The Grid Coefficient Rule · Solving the Equations

Use when: two routes reach the same point and contain unknown multipliers. Think of a and b as two independent grid directions. If a and b are non-parallel, their coefficients must match separately.

\[p\mathbf a+q\mathbf b=r\mathbf a+s\mathbf b\quad\Rightarrow\quad p=r,\ q=s.\]

WORKED EXAMPLE · TWO ROUTES, ONE POINT

Let OA = a, OB = b and X lie on AB. Also OX = t(a + b). Write AX = λAB:

\[\overrightarrow{OX}=\mathbf a+\lambda(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)=(1-\lambda)\mathbf a+\lambda\mathbf b.\]

Compare this with ta + tb. Match a: 1 − λ = t. Match b: λ = t. Therefore 1 − t = t, so t = λ = ½. X is the midpoint of AB.

Trap: if a and b are parallel, you cannot assume their coefficients match independently.

Try it 3 · Find the unknown coefficients

Question: For non-parallel a and b, (2p + q)a + (p − q)b = 7a + 2b. Find p and q.

Reveal worked answer

Match coefficients: 2p + q = 7 and p − q = 2. Add: 3p = 9, so p = 3. Then q = 1. Substitute back: 2(3) + 1 = 7 and 3 − 1 = 2.

↗️ Vector Parallelism Check

Use when: asked whether two directions are parallel or to find an unknown that makes them parallel. For two non-zero vectors, every component must use the same multiplier.

\[\mathbf v=k\mathbf u\quad(k\ne0).\]

In 2D, an equivalent check for non-zero u = (u₁,u₂) and v = (v₁,v₂) is \(u_1v_2-u_2v_1=0\). This avoids dividing by a zero component.

WORKED EXAMPLE · ONE MULTIPLIER FOR BOTH COMPONENTS

u = (2,−3) and v = (−6,9): v = −3u, so they are parallel in opposite directions. But w = (6,−8) is not parallel to u: the first component needs ×3, while the second needs ×⁸⁄₃.

Unknown example: (4,k) parallel to (6,9) gives scale 4/6 = ⅔, so k = ⅔ × 9 = 6. Check: 4×9 − 6×6 = 0.

Try it 4 · Parallel or not?

Question: (a) Are (3,−2) and (−12,8) parallel? (b) Find k so (k,10) is parallel to (3,5).

Reveal worked answer

(a) Yes: (−12,8) = −4(3,−2); opposite directions. (b) Second component gives multiplier 10/5 = 2, so k = 2×3 = 6.

20-second recall: Route → add arrows. Reverse → change sign. Straight line → same bracket + shared point. Unknowns → match independent coefficients. Parallel → one multiplier for every component.

9. Indices, surds, number and statistics

Index laws

\[ a^ma^n=a^{m+n} \qquad \frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n} \]

\[ (a^m)^n=a^{mn} \qquad a^{-n}=\frac1{a^n} \]

\[ a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a} \qquad a^{m/n}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^m \]

\[ (ab)^n=a^nb^n \]

Warning: \((a+b)^2\ne a^2+b^2\).

Surds

\[ \sqrt{ab}=\sqrt a\sqrt b \]

\[ \sqrt{48}=4\sqrt3 \]

To rationalise:

\[ \frac1{\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{3} \]

For denominator \(a+\sqrt b\), multiply by its conjugate \(a-\sqrt b\).

Number techniques

  • Standard form: \(a\times10^n\), with \(1\le|a|<10\).
  • Percentage multiplier: \(1\pm r/100\).
  • Reverse percentage: divide final amount by multiplier.
  • Repeated percentage change: use multiplier raised to the number of periods.
  • Bounds: use half the rounding interval above and below the stated value.
  • Ratio sharing: total ÷ sum of ratio parts, then multiply by each part.
  • HCF: lowest common prime powers.
  • LCM: highest prime powers.

Statistics

\[ \text{Mean}=\frac{\sum fx}{\sum f} \]

Grouped mean: use class midpoints as \(x\).

\[ \text{Frequency density}= \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{class width}} \]

\[ \text{Pie chart angle}= \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total}}\times360^\circ \]

  • Median: middle ordered value.
  • Mode: most frequent value.
  • Modal class: class with highest frequency, not necessarily highest histogram density.
  • Histogram: frequency is proportional to bar area, not just height.
  • Estimate of grouped mean: use class midpoints.
  • Missing frequency: form an equation using the given total or mean.
WHEN TO USE IT · WORKED EXAMPLE

Number & statistics · common exam uses

Indices: 8^(2/3) = (∛8)² = 4. Rationalise 1/(2 + √3) = 2 − √3. A price after a 20% rise is £72 → original = 72/1.2 = £60. £100 growing 5% for 2 years becomes 100×1.05² = £110.25. Rounded to nearest 0.1: 4.2 means 4.15 ≤ x < 4.25. Share 60 in ratio 2:3 → 24 and 36. HCF(12,18) = 6; LCM = 36. Frequencies 2 and 3 at values 1 and 4 give mean (2×1 + 3×4)/5 = 2.8. A histogram class of width 5 and frequency 20 has density 4; a pie-chart frequency 15 out of 60 gives 90°.
