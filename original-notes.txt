## 1. Algebraic fractions — hardest expressions

High-priority method

Whenever you see huge algebraic fractions, cubics, repeated brackets, or denominators such as \\((3x-2)^2\\), follow this sequence.

FACTOR → REWRITE → CANCEL → SIMPLIFY → CHECK RESTRICTIONS

### Recognise these identities instantly

| Expression          | Factorised form         |
| ------------------- | ----------------------- |
| \\(a^2-b^2\\)       | \\((a-b)(a+b)\\)        |
| \\(a^2+2ab+b^2\\)   | \\((a+b)^2\\)           |
| \\(a^2-2ab+b^2\\)   | \\((a-b)^2\\)           |
| \\(a^3-b^3\\)       | \\((a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)\\) |
| \\(a^3+b^3\\)       | \\((a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)\\) |
| \\(x^2+(p+q)x+pq\\) | \\((x+p)(x+q)\\)        |

Remember: minus between two cubes produces plus in the middle of the quadratic factor.

### Worked example: a large fraction with cubes

Simplify:

\\[ \frac{27x^3-8}{9x^2-12x+4}\times\frac{3x-2}{3x+1} \\]

Step 1 — Factor both expressions.

\\[ 27x^3-8=(3x-2)(9x^2+6x+4) \\]

\\[ 9x^2-12x+4=(3x-2)^2 \\]

Step 2 — Rewrite the entire fraction.

\\[ \frac{(3x-2)(9x^2+6x+4)}{(3x-2)^2} \times\frac{3x-2}{3x+1} \\]

Step 3 — Cancel common factors.

There are two factors of \\((3x-2)\\) above and two below.

\\[ \boxed{\frac{9x^2+6x+4}{3x+1}} \\]

Restrictions from the original denominator:

\\[ x\ne\frac23,\qquad x\ne-\frac13 \\]

Never forget:

- \\((3x-2)(3x-2)=(3x-2)^2\\), not \\(9x^2-4\\).
- You cancel factors, not individual terms joined by addition or subtraction.
- You may cancel \\((x+2)\\) against \\((x+2)\\), but cannot cancel \\(x\\) from \\(\frac{x+2}{x+3}\\).
- A factor removed by cancellation still gives an excluded value of \\(x\\).

### Adding and subtracting algebraic fractions

Example:

\\[ \frac{2}{3x-2}-\frac{x+1}{(3x-2)^2} \\]

The lowest common denominator is \\((3x-2)^2\\).

\\[ =\frac{2(3x-2)-(x+1)}{(3x-2)^2} \\]

\\[ =\boxed{\frac{5x-5}{(3x-2)^2}} \\]

Watch the minus sign: \\(-(x+1)=-x-1\\).

Rules for every operation:

| Operation           | What to do                                              |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Addition            | Find common denominator, then add numerators            |
| Subtraction         | Find common denominator, subtract the entire numerator  |
| Multiplication      | Factor, cancel, multiply                                |
| Division            | Flip second fraction, then multiply                     |
| Solving an equation | State restrictions, clear denominators, solve and check |

## 2. Quadratics, cubics and equations

### Quadratic formula

For \\(ax^2+bx+c=0\\):

\\[ \boxed{x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}} \\]

Discriminant \\(D=b^2-4ac\\):

- \\(D>0\\): two different real roots.
- \\(D=0\\): one repeated real root.
- \\(D<0\\): no real roots.

### Completing the square

\\[ x^2+bx+c= \left(x+\frac b2\right)^2+c-\frac{b^2}{4} \\]

Example:

\\[ x^2-6x+5=(x-3)^2-4 \\]

Technique: halve the coefficient of \\(x\\), square it, then adjust the constant.

For \\(ax^2+bx+c\\), factor out \\(a\\) from the first two terms before completing the square.

### Cubic equations and factor theorem

For a cubic \\(f(x)\\):

1. Test a possible root \\(r\\).
2. If \\(f(r)=0\\), then \\((x-r)\\) is a factor.
3. Divide the cubic by \\((x-r)\\).
4. Factorise or solve the remaining quadratic.

For \\((ax-b)\\), test \\(x=b/a\\), not \\(x=b\\).

Important identities:

\\[ x^3-y^3=(x-y)(x^2+xy+y^2) \\]

\\[ x^3+y^3=(x+y)(x^2-xy+y^2) \\]

### Simultaneous equations

Two linear equations: elimination or substitution.

One linear + one quadratic:

1. Rearrange the linear equation.
2. Substitute into the quadratic.
3. Expand and collect terms.
4. Solve the resulting quadratic.
5. Substitute both answers into the linear equation.

Never give one solution if the quadratic produces two valid pairs.

### Inequalities

For \\(x^2-5x+4<0\\):

\\[ (x-1)(x-4)<0 \\]

Roots are \\(1\\) and \\(4\\). Since the parabola opens upwards, it is negative between the roots.

\\[ \boxed{1\<x<4} \\]

Remember:

- \\(\lt,\gt\\): open-circle endpoints.
- \\(\le,\ge\\): filled-circle endpoints.
- Divide by a negative: reverse the inequality.
- Quadratic inequalities: find roots, then use signs or a sketch.

Shaded regions: rearrange to \\(y\\), use solid lines for inclusive inequalities, dashed lines for strict ones, then test a point such as \\((0,0)\\) if it is not on the boundary.

## 3. Differentiation — stationary points, tangents and normals

Essential

### The power rule

\\[ \boxed{\frac{d}{dx}(ax^n)=anx^{n-1}} \\]

Examples:

| Original     | Derivative     |
| ------------ | -------------- |
| \\(x^4\\)    | \\(4x^3\\)     |
| \\(5x^3\\)   | \\(15x^2\\)    |
| \\(-7x^2\\)  | \\(-14x\\)     |
| \\(6x\\)     | \\(6\\)        |
| \\(12\\)     | \\(0\\)        |
| \\(x^{-2}\\) | \\(-2x^{-3}\\) |

Differentiate every term separately. Constants become zero.

### Stationary points — full technique

Given:

\\[ y=x^3-6x^2+9x+1 \\]

Step 1 — Differentiate.

\\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=3x^2-12x+9 \\]

Step 2 — Set derivative equal to zero.

\\[ 3x^2-12x+9=0 \\]

\\[ 3(x-1)(x-3)=0 \\]

Thus \\(x=1\\) or \\(x=3\\).

Step 3 — Substitute into the ORIGINAL equation.

\\[ y(1)=5,\quad y(3)=1 \\]

Stationary points are:

\\[ \boxed{(1,5)\text{ and }(3,1)} \\]

Step 4 — Classify them.

Use the second derivative:

\\[ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=6x-12 \\]

- At \\(x=1\\), second derivative is negative → local maximum.
- At \\(x=3\\), second derivative is positive → local minimum.

Therefore \\((1,5)\\) is a maximum and \\((3,1)\\) is a minimum.

If the second derivative is zero, this test is inconclusive; check the gradient's sign on both sides.

### Tangents and normals

The derivative at a point is the tangent gradient.

For a tangent through \\((x_1,y_1)\\):

\\[ \boxed{y-y_1=m(x-x_1)} \\]

The normal is perpendicular:

\\[ m\_{\text{normal}}=-\frac{1}{m\_{\text{tangent}}} \\]

If the tangent gradient is zero, the normal is vertical.

Technique: differentiate → substitute the given \\(x\\) → find the gradient → calculate \\(y\\) → use the straight-line formula.

### Motion questions

\\[ v=\frac{ds}{dt},\qquad a=\frac{dv}{dt} \\]

- Displacement → differentiate → velocity.
- Velocity → differentiate → acceleration.
- At rest → set \\(v=0\\).
- Speed is the magnitude \\(|v|\\), not negative velocity.
- Distance–time gradient = speed.
- Displacement–time gradient = velocity.
- Velocity–time gradient = acceleration.
- Area under velocity–time graph = displacement; account for negative regions.
- Area under speed–time graph = distance.

Do not forget: Stationary means zero gradient. It does not automatically mean maximum or minimum.

## 4. Probability — every major question type

Pearson specifically includes probability trees, independent events, mutually exclusive events, simple conditional probability, complements, and expected frequency.&#x20;

### Master probability rules

| Question wording                  | Rule                                                   |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Probability of A                  | Favourable outcomes ÷ total equally likely outcomes    |
| NOT A                             | \\(1-P(A)\\)                                           |
| A AND B, independent              | \\(P(A)\times P(B)\\)                                  |
| A OR B, mutually exclusive        | \\(P(A)+P(B)\\)                                        |
| A OR B, overlapping               | \\(P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\\)                             |
| At least one success              | \\(1-P(\text{none})\\)                                 |
| Exactly one success in two trials | Add the two paths: success–failure and failure–success |
| Expected frequency                | Probability × number of trials                         |
| Experimental probability          | Frequency observed ÷ number of trials                  |
| Given that B happened             | Favourable outcomes within B ÷ total outcomes within B |

### With and without replacement

A bag contains 3 red and 2 blue balls.

With replacement: probabilities remain unchanged after the first draw.

\\[ P(RR)=\frac35\times\frac35=\frac9{25} \\]

Without replacement: the denominator decreases because one ball is gone.

\\[ P(RR)=\frac35\times\frac24=\frac3{10} \\]

For exactly one red:

\\[ P(RB)+P(BR) \\]

\\[ =\frac35\times\frac24+\frac25\times\frac34 =\boxed{\frac35} \\]

For at least one red:

\\[ 1-P(BB)=1-\frac25\times\frac14 =\boxed{\frac9{10}} \\]

Remember: For tree diagrams, multiply along each complete path, then add the separate paths that satisfy the question.

### Conditional probability

If the question says "given that the first ball was red", the first selection is already known.

\\[ P(\text{second red given first red})=\frac24 \\]

Do not multiply by \\(3/5\\) again.

For two events:

\\[ P(A\text{ given }B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)} \\]

### Venn diagrams and sets

| Symbol             | Meaning                  |
| ------------------ | ------------------------ |
| \\(A\cup B\\)      | A OR B, including both   |
| \\(A\cap B\\)      | A AND B, common elements |
| \\(A'\\)           | NOT A, complement        |
| \\(A\subseteq B\\) | A is a subset of B       |
| \\(n(A)\\)         | Number of elements in A  |
| \\(\varnothing\\)  | Empty set                |
| \\(\xi\\)          | Universal set            |
| \\(A\setminus B\\) | A but not B              |

\\[ n(A\cup B)=n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B) \\]

Venn technique: Fill the middle intersection first, then the exclusive regions, and finally the outside. Check that everything totals the universal set.

If you see \\((A\cup B)'\\), perform the operation inside the brackets first, then take the complement.

### Other probability traps

- Independent does not mean mutually exclusive.
- Probabilities of all possible outcomes sum to 1.
- For a fair six-sided die, each individual number has probability \\(1/6\\).
- "At least two" includes two, three, four, etc.
- "More than two" excludes two.
- For unordered pairs, count both possible orders unless the problem's outcomes are already defined as unordered.

## 5. Matrices — every essential operation

Let

\\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \\]

| Operation             | Rule                                                        |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Addition              | Add matching positions                                      |
| Subtraction           | Subtract matching positions                                 |
| Scalar multiplication | Multiply every entry                                        |
| Matrix multiplication | Row × column                                                |
| Determinant           | \\(ad-bc\\)                                                 |
| Inverse               | \\(\frac1{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}\\) |

### Matrix multiplication

\\[ \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}e&f\\\g&h\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ae+bg&af+bh\\\ ce+dg&cf+dh \end{pmatrix} \\]

Never multiply corresponding entries for ordinary matrix multiplication.

- \\(AB\ne BA\\) in general.
- \\(A(B+C)=AB+AC\\).
- \\(AI=IA=A\\).
- \\(A^{-1}A=I\\).
- \\(A^{-1}\\) exists for a \\(2\times2\\) matrix only when \\(ad-bc\ne0\\).
- The number of columns of the first matrix must equal the number of rows of the second.

Solving matrix equations: If \\(AX=B\\), multiply on the left by \\(A^{-1}\\), giving \\(X=A^{-1}B\\).

### Transformation matrices

| Transformation             | Matrix                                        |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Reflection in x-axis       | \\(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\\)  |
| Reflection in y-axis       | \\(\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\\)  |
| Reflection in \\(y=x\\)    | \\(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\\)   |
| 90° anticlockwise          | \\(\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\\)  |
| 90° clockwise              | \\(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\\) |
| 180° rotation              | \\(\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\\) |
| Enlargement, scale \\(k\\) | \\(\begin{pmatrix}k&0\\\0&k\end{pmatrix}\\)   |

For combined transformations, the right-hand matrix acts first.

## 6. Functions, sequences and graphs

### Function notation

If \\(f(x)=2x+3\\), then \\(f(4)=11\\).

For composite functions:

\\[ fg(x)=f(g(x)) \\]

Always do the inside function first.

For an inverse:

1. Replace \\(f(x)\\) with \\(y\\).
2. Swap \\(x\\) and \\(y\\).
3. Rearrange for \\(y\\).
4. Write \\(f^{-1}(x)\\).

Domain: permissible input values. Range: possible outputs.

Exclude any value making a denominator zero.

### Straight-line graphs

\\[ y=mx+c,\qquad m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1} \\]

- Parallel lines: same gradient.
- Perpendicular lines: product of gradients \\(=-1\\), when both gradients are defined.
- At the intersection: both equations have the same \\(x,y\\).
- x-intercept: set \\(y=0\\).
- y-intercept: set \\(x=0\\).

### Sequences

Arithmetic sequence:

\\[ u_n=a+(n-1)d \\]

Where \\(a\\) is the first term and \\(d\\) is the common difference.

For quadratic sequences, find the constant second difference:

\\[ u_n=an^2+bn+c \\]

The constant second difference is \\(2a\\).

### Proportion

- \\(y\propto x\\) → \\(y=kx\\)
- \\(y\propto x^2\\) → \\(y=kx^2\\)
- \\(y\propto 1/x\\) → \\(y=k/x\\)
- \\(y\propto 1/x^2\\) → \\(y=k/x^2\\)

Find \\(k\\) first, using the supplied values.

## 7. Geometry and trigonometry — formula recap

### Trigonometry

\\[ \sin\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \\]

\\[ \cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}} \\]

\\[ \tan\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} \\]

For non-right triangles:

\\[ \frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C} \\]

\\[ a^2=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A \\]

\\[ \text{Area}=\frac12 ab\sin C \\]

Remember:

- Sine rule: opposite side–angle pairs.
- Cosine rule: three sides, or two sides and their included angle.
- Check the ambiguous case when using the sine rule to find an angle.
- Calculator must be in DEG mode.
- Bearings are measured clockwise from North, using three figures.

### Circle theorems

| Situation                               | Rule                                 |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Angle at centre vs circumference        | Centre = 2 × circumference           |
| Angles in same segment                  | Equal                                |
| Angle in semicircle                     | 90°                                  |
| Opposite angles of cyclic quadrilateral | Sum to 180°                          |
| Radius meets tangent                    | 90°                                  |
| Tangents from same external point       | Equal lengths                        |
| Tangent–chord angle                     | Equal to angle in alternate segment  |
| Perpendicular from centre to chord      | Bisects chord                        |
| Two intersecting chords                 | Products of their segments are equal |

For external point P with secants PAB and PCD:

\\[ PA\times PB=PC\times PD \\]

For tangent PT and secant PAB:

\\[ PT^2=PA\times PB \\]

### Congruence vs similarity

- Congruent: exactly the same size and shape.
- Similar: same shape, possibly different size.
- Congruence tests: SSS, SAS, ASA, RHS.
- AAA proves similarity, not necessarily congruence.
- SSA generally does not prove congruence.

For similar shapes with linear scale factor \\(k\\):

\\[ \text{Area factor}=k^2,\qquad \text{Volume factor}=k^3 \\]

### Mensuration

| Shape      | Essential formula                                      |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Triangle   | \\(\frac12 bh\\)                                       |
| Trapezium  | \\(\frac12(a+b)h\\), where a,b are parallel sides      |
| Circle     | \\(A=\pi r^2,\ C=2\pi r\\)                             |
| Sector     | \\(A=\frac{\theta}{360}\pi r^2\\)                      |
| Arc length | \\(L=\frac{\theta}{360}2\pi r\\)                       |
| Cylinder   | \\(V=\pi r^2h\\)                                       |
| Cone       | \\(V=\frac13\pi r^2h\\)                                |
| Sphere     | \\(V=\frac43\pi r^3\\)                                 |
| Prism      | \\(V=\text{cross-sectional area}\times\text{length}\\) |
| Pyramid    | \\(V=\frac13\times\text{base area}\times h\\)          |

Surface areas:

\\[ \text{Cylinder}=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh \\]

\\[ \text{Sphere}=4\pi r^2 \\]

\\[ \text{Cone}=\pi r^2+\pi rl \\]

Here \\(l\\) is the cone's slant height.

3D cuboid diagonal:

\\[ d=\sqrt{l^2+w^2+h^2} \\]

Composite shapes: Split into basic shapes, calculate separately, then add or subtract. Don't include hidden/internal faces when calculating external surface area.

## 8. Vectors

For points \\(A\\) and \\(B\\):

\\[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf b-\mathbf a \\]

For \\(\mathbf v=\begin{pmatrix}a\\\b\end{pmatrix}\\):

\\[ |\mathbf v|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2} \\]

Unit vector:

\\[ \frac{\mathbf v}{|\mathbf v|} \\]

Vector proof rules:

- Parallel vectors are scalar multiples.
- Collinear points lie on the same straight line.
- A midpoint divides a vector into two equal vectors.
- If \\(M\\) divides \\(AB\\) in the ratio \\(p:q\\), then

\\[ \overrightarrow{AM}=\frac{p}{p+q}\overrightarrow{AB} \\]

Remember: Draw arrows and follow direction. \\(\overrightarrow{BA}=-\overrightarrow{AB}\\).

## 9. Indices, surds, number and statistics

### Index laws

\\[ a^ma^n=a^{m+n} \qquad \frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n} \\]

\\[ (a^m)^n=a^{mn} \qquad a^{-n}=\frac1{a^n} \\]

\\[ a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a} \qquad a^{m/n}=(\sqrt[n]{a})^m \\]

\\[ (ab)^n=a^nb^n \\]

Warning: \\((a+b)^2\ne a^2+b^2\\).

### Surds

\\[ \sqrt{ab}=\sqrt a\sqrt b \\]

\\[ \sqrt{48}=4\sqrt3 \\]

To rationalise:

\\[ \frac1{\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{3} \\]

For denominator \\(a+\sqrt b\\), multiply by its conjugate \\(a-\sqrt b\\).

### Number techniques

- Standard form: \\(a\times10^n\\), with \\(1\le|a|<10\\).
- Percentage multiplier: \\(1\pm r/100\\).
- Reverse percentage: divide final amount by multiplier.
- Repeated percentage change: use multiplier raised to the number of periods.
- Bounds: use half the rounding interval above and below the stated value.
- Ratio sharing: total ÷ sum of ratio parts, then multiply by each part.
- HCF: lowest common prime powers.
- LCM: highest prime powers.

### Statistics

\\[ \text{Mean}=\frac{\sum fx}{\sum f} \\]

Grouped mean: use class midpoints as \\(x\\).

\\[ \text{Frequency density}= \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{class width}} \\]

\\[ \text{Pie chart angle}= \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total}}\times360^\circ \\]

- Median: middle ordered value.
- Mode: most frequent value.
- Modal class: class with highest frequency, not necessarily highest histogram density.
- Histogram: frequency is proportional to bar area, not just height.
- Estimate of grouped mean: use class midpoints.
- Missing frequency: form an equation using the given total or mean.

Final accuracy checks

Factor expressions before cancelling

Use original function for stationary-point y-coordinates

Classify stationary points; give tangent or normal equation if requested

The most important exam technique: If a question looks complicated, identify which mathematical operation it is asking for before calculating. A huge algebraic fraction may require only factorisation; a long probability question may require only the complement rule; and a difficult-looking differentiation problem often becomes a quadratic after setting the derivative to zero.
